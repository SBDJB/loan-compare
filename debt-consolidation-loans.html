Debt consolidation loans: when they help and when they don't

Consolidating means taking one personal loan to pay off several debts. It can save real money, but only when the numbers line up.

How it works

You take out a personal loan, use it to pay off your credit cards or other debts, and then repay the loan in fixed monthly instalments. You are left with one payment, one interest rate and a known end date.

Nothing is forgiven. You owe the same amount as before, to a different lender, on different terms. Whether that is an improvement depends entirely on those terms.

When it helps

  • The loan's APR is clearly lower than the average rate on the debts it replaces.
  • You want a finish line. A credit card has no end date if you pay only a little more than the minimum. A loan does.
  • One payment is easier to manage than several due dates, so you are less likely to miss one.

A worked example

Say you owe $10,000 on a credit card at 24% APR and can afford about $350 a month.

Keep paying the card36-month loan at 14% APR
Monthly payment$350.00$341.78
Time to pay off43 months36 months
Total interest$4,976.08$2,303.95

For roughly the same monthly payment, the loan clears the debt 7 months sooner and saves about $2,672 in interest. This example assumes the loan has no origination fee and that you stop adding to the card.

When it does not help

  • The rate is not lower. With weaker credit, the loan offered may carry a rate close to or above your cards. Then you gain a single payment and nothing else.
  • A fee eats the saving. An origination fee is charged upfront. Compare the loan's APR, which includes the fee, with your card rates. See personal loan fees explained.
  • A longer term raises the total. The same $10,000 at 14% over 60 months has a payment of $232.68 but costs $3,960.95 in interest, compared with $2,303.95 over 36 months.
  • The cards fill up again. This is the most common way consolidation goes wrong. Paying off the cards frees their credit limits. If you run them back up, you have the loan and the card debt.

Before you consolidate

  1. List each debt with its balance, APR and minimum payment.
  2. Work out what you pay in total each month now.
  3. Prequalify with a few lenders to see the APR you would actually get. This uses a soft credit check. See prequalification versus applying.
  4. Compare total interest on the loan with total interest if you kept paying the same amount on your current debts.
  5. Decide what you will do with the paid-off cards. Keeping them open and unused generally helps your credit score more than closing them.

Alternatives worth checking

  • Balance transfer card. Some cards offer a low or 0% introductory rate on transferred balances for a limited period, usually with a transfer fee. This can beat a loan if you can clear the balance before the introductory period ends.
  • Paying the highest-rate debt first. Put every spare dollar toward the debt with the highest APR while paying minimums on the rest. It costs nothing to set up.
  • Non-profit credit counseling. A counselor can review your budget and may arrange a debt management plan with reduced rates from your card issuers.
