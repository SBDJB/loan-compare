Fixed versus variable rate loans

Most personal loans have a fixed rate. Some lenders offer variable rates that start lower and can move. Here is what you are trading.

Fixed rate

The interest rate is set when you take the loan and stays the same until it is repaid. Every payment is the same amount, and you know from day one exactly what the loan will cost.

Variable rate

The interest rate is tied to a published benchmark rate plus a margin set by the lender. When the benchmark rises or falls, your rate follows, usually at set intervals. Your payment, the length of the loan, or both can change as a result.

Variable rates often start below the fixed rate the same lender would offer. In exchange, you carry the risk that rates go up.

What a rate rise costs

Take a $10,000 loan over 60 months.

  • At 10% the payment is $212.47 a month.
  • At 13% the payment is $227.53 a month.

A three-point rise adds about $15.06 to each payment. If it applied for the whole term, that would be about $904 more in total. The longer the loan, the more time there is for rates to move and the more a change costs.

Questions to ask about a variable rate

  1. Which benchmark is the rate tied to, and what is the lender's margin?
  2. How often can the rate change?
  3. Is there a cap on how much it can rise at each change, and over the life of the loan?
  4. When the rate changes, does my payment change, or does the term?
  5. Can I switch to a fixed rate later, and at what cost?

Which to choose

A fixed rate usually suits you if:

  • your budget is tight and a higher payment would be a problem;
  • the loan runs for several years;
  • you value knowing the total cost in advance.

A variable rate can make sense if:

  • the starting rate is meaningfully lower;
  • you expect to repay the loan quickly, leaving little time for rates to rise;
  • you could comfortably afford the payment at the rate cap.

A simple test. Work out the payment at the highest rate the loan allows. If you could not afford that, choose the fixed rate.
